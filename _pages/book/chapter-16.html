Reasoning in LLMs

18 min read 14 sections Draft

16.1Reasoning

Reasoning means the ability to think through a problem step-by-step, connect facts, and arrive at a logical conclusion — rather than just recalling information. It involves:

  • Understanding relationships between ideas,

  • Making inferences,

  • Evaluating evidence, and

  • Drawing conclusions that are consistent with logic.

In humans, reasoning is what we use when we solve math problems, analyze cause and effect, or plan actions.

16.2Reasoning in LLMs

In the context of Large Language Models (LLMs) like GPT, Claude, or Gemini, Reasoning refers to the model’s ability to perform multi-step thinking before producing a final answer.

So a reasoning LLM is one that

  • Breaks down problems into smaller steps,

  • Performs internal logic or calculation,

  • Explains or justifies its answers (often via “chain-of-thought”), and

  • Can generalize to new reasoning tasks (math, planning, science, coding, etc.).

16.3Reasoning Models

A reasoning model is a type of LLM that is specifically trained or fine-tuned to reason explicitly instead of just predicting text fluently.

They are optimized not just for “what sounds right” but for “what makes logical sense.”

Features of Reasoning Models

  • Trained on chain-of-thought (CoT) data — text where humans or other models show step-by-step reasoning.

  • Often use intermediate reasoning traces (like scratchpads) before answering.

  • May use reinforcement learning or distillation to encourage correct reasoning steps.

  • Sometimes fine-tuned on structured tasks like math proofs, programming logic, or symbolic reasoning.

Example

Prompt: What is (3 + 2) × (4 - 1)?

A normal (non-reasoning) LLM might

  • Memorize patterns like “math question → give final number.”

  • Output directly: “15”

A reasoning LLM would

Use chain-of-thought reasoning internally

Step 1: Compute (3 + 2) = 5

Step 2: Compute (4 - 1) = 3

Step 3: Multiply 5 × 3 = 15

Final Answer: 15

Feature Standard LLM Reasoning Model
Goal Predict next word Solve problems logically
Training Plain text prediction Fine-tuned on reasoning traces
Strength Fluency, general knowledge Step-by-step thinking, problem solving
Example Use Chatting, summarization Math, coding, planning, logical inference

16.4Reasoning-based benchmarks

Reasoning-based benchmarks are specially designed evaluation datasets used to measure how well a model can think through problems, not just recall information.

They test whether a model can

  • Understand cause and effect,

  • Perform step-by-step logic,

  • Solve math or science problems,

  • Plan or deduce, and

  • Generalize reasoning to new contexts.

So, instead of asking factual or knowledge-based questions (like “Who wrote Hamlet?”), These benchmarks ask multi-step reasoning questions (like “If all poets are writers, and Shakespeare is a poet, is Shakespeare a writer?”).

Reasoning benchmarks are designed to

  • Detect true reasoning ability, not memorization.

  • Evaluate internal logic — can the model connect multiple facts correctly?

  • Track progress as models evolve (e.g., from GPT-3 → GPT-4 → reasoning models like o1 or Gemini 2).

Step 1: Define Reasoning Type

Researchers decide what kind of reasoning they want to test.
Common categories include:

  • Arithmetic reasoning (math word problems)

  • Symbolic reasoning (logic puzzles)

  • Commonsense reasoning (daily cause-effect)

  • Scientific reasoning (physics, chemistry)

  • Multi-hop reasoning (connecting multiple facts across paragraphs)

  • Planning or deductive reasoning (multi-step decision-making)

Step 2: Collect or Generate Questions

There are two main ways

  • Human-created:
    Experts or crowdsourced annotators manually design reasoning questions with correct answers and reasoning chains.

  • (b) Automatically generated:
    Scripts or other models generate problems (e.g., math equations, logic chains), ensuring they need multi-step deduction to solve.

Step 3: Verify Reasoning Path

Each question is checked to make sure it

  • Cannot be solved by simple pattern matching or keyword lookup.

  • Requires actual logical or step-by-step thinking.

  • Has one clear, consistent answer.
    Some datasets even include gold-standard reasoning traces (showing the correct step-by-step logic).

Examples of Reasoning-Based Benchmarks

Example Question (from GSM8K)

Q: A train travels 60 km in 1.5 hours. What is its average speed in km/h?

To solve

  • Step 1: Distance = 60 km

  • Step 2: Time = 1.5 hours

  • Step 3: Speed = Distance / Time = 60 / 1.5 = 40 km/h

Answer: 40 km/h

16.5Pass@k

Pass@k is a metric used to evaluate how often a language model produces a correct answer within its top-k generated outputs.

If we ask a model the same question N times (using different sampling randomness), and it gets the correct answer in C of those samples, then:

Pass@1=NC​

means “what fraction of samples had a correct answer in the first (top-1) generation.”

But we can also check

Pass@k=Probability that at least one of the top-k samples is correct.

So even if the model makes some mistakes, it “passes” if any of the top-k generations are right.

Let

  • N: total samples per problem

  • ci​: number of correct generations for the problem i

  • k: number of top samples we consider

Then (for a dataset of size M):

Pass@k=M1​i=1∑M​[1−(kN​)(kN−ci​​)​]

This is the expected probability that at least one of the kchosen outputs for the problem iis correct.

Example of Pass@k

Imagine we ask an LLM to solve a math problem like: “What is 23 × 19?”

We sample N = 5 outputs from the model (different random seeds or temperatures)

Sample Model Output Correct?
#1 437 ✅ Correct
#2 432 ❌
#3 435 ❌
#4 437 ✅ Correct
#5 420 ❌

So there are C=2correct generations out of 5.

Pass@1

If we only take the first sample (k = 1)

Pass@1=51​=0.2=20%

Pass@3

What’s the chance that at least one of the top-3 generations is correct?

You can compute it as

Pass@3=1−(35​)(35−2​)​=1−(35​)(33​)​=1−101​=0.9
So Pass@3 = 90%

That means if we look at the top-3 generated outputs per question, the model solves 90% of problems correctly.

Interpretation

  • Pass@1 = model’s accuracy when giving one shot (greedy output).

  • Pass@k = accuracy when you allow the model k attempts and accept any correct one.

Higher k→ more chances to include a correct answer.

16.6How Temperature Affects Pass@k

Temperature controls the randomness or diversity of model outputs during sampling.

Temperature Behavior Effect on Pass@k
0.0 Deterministic (always same output) Pass@1 = Pass@k (no diversity)
0.5 Moderate randomness Increases diversity; higher Pass@k possible
1.0+ Very random / creative Outputs vary a lot; Pass@k may improve (diversity) but Pass@1 may drop (less reliable)

16.7Cons@k

Cons@k measures how consistent a model’s answers are when you ask the same question multiple times and get k different responses (using random sampling).

It tells you: “Out of k generated answers, how often do they agree with each other (give the same answer or reasoning)?”

16.8Scaling with RL

Use reinforcement learning (RL) to encourage the model to think (reason) before answering.

Key Points

  • Writing full reasoning chains by hand is hard and impractical.

  • The model should learn its own reasoning, not just copy human examples.

  • Use verifiable rewards — check if the answer is correct (“yes/no”).

Rewards

  • Check if reasoning (chain-of-thought) is present.

  • Check if the final solution is correct.
    → Reward = reasoning format + accuracy.

Control Thinking

  • Not all prompts need the same reasoning depth.

  • Adjust reasoning using:

    • Dynamic budget (vary thinking time).

    • Context awareness (think more for harder questions).

    • Budget forcing or continuous thoughts to manage reasoning length.

16.9GRPO: Group Relative Policy Optimization

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) is a reinforcement learning algorithm that fine-tunes a model’s policy by comparing multiple outputs (a group) for the same input and updating the policy based on relative performance within that group, rather than training a separate critic (value) model like PPO does.

Why GRPO? (Motivation)

  • No critic model needed: Removes the extra value network, saving compute and memory.

  • Group comparisons: By evaluating several outputs together, it reduces noisy or sparse rewards and improves stability.

  • Efficient training: Especially useful when rewards are sparse or hard to estimate directly (e.g., reasoning tasks).

16.10Increasing Length

The “increasing length” problem in RL-based language model training. The issue is that longer outputs get more total reward simply because they have more tokens, not because they are better. This biases the model to generate unnecessarily long responses.

G1​i=1∑G​∣oi​∣1​t=1∑∣oi​∣​

  • The part ∣oi​∣1​is length normalization.

  • Without it, longer sequences contribute more reward.

  • With it, the total reward is averaged over tokens, so each token contributes equally, regardless of sequence length.

  • In short: it removes the advantage of being long.

The solution is to equalize token-level contributions

  • Instead of rewarding whole sequences, reward is distributed evenly across tokens.

  • This is what Dr. GRPO does, compared to standard GRPO.

Dr. GRPO removes the length bias in standard GRPO by making each token contribute equally to the reward, instead of letting longer outputs accumulate more reward.

Key idea (math intuition)

  • Standard GRPO rewards the whole sequence → longer = more reward.

  • Dr. GRPO changes this to token-level averaging:

G1​i=1∑G​t=1∑∣oi​∣​

  • No division by output length at the sequence level

  • Reward is treated per token, not per sequence

  • Each token has equal weight, regardless of how long the output is

Ai,t​=G1​∑j=1G​(R(q,oj​)−G1​∑k=1G​R(q,ok​))2​R(q,oi​)−G1​∑j=1G​R(q,oj​)​

It normalizes the reward by the mean and standard deviation across sampled outputs, removing bias caused by question difficulty and making learning signals comparable.

16.11.2Asymmetric PPO clipping (for diversity)

clip ⁣(ri,t​(θ), 1−εlow​, 1+εhigh​)

It uses asymmetric clipping so the policy can explore more in one direction, encouraging diverse outputs instead of collapsing to similar answers.

16.12DeepSeek R1-Zero’s training

DeepSeek R1-Zero is trained in two main stages

  • Pretraining a strong base language model (V3-Base)

  • Teaching the model reasoning using reinforcement learning (GRPO) → R1-Zero

Step 1: Pretrain model with traditional techniques → V3-Base

In this step, DeepSeek first trains a base language model using standard LLM training (next-token prediction on large text data).

The goal is to make the model good at language: understanding text, grammar, facts, and basic patterns.

This pretrained model is called V3-Base. It is not a reasoning model yet, but it provides a strong foundation needed before teaching advanced reasoning.

Step 2: GRPO with reasoning data → R1-Zero

Next, the V3-Base model is trained using reinforcement learning (GRPO) on reasoning tasks like math and logic problems.

The model generates multiple answers, compares them, and is rewarded for better reasoning and correct solutions. Through this process, the model learns how to reason step by step by itself, without using human-written chain-of-thought examples. After this stage, the model becomes DeepSeek R1-Zero, a strong reasoning-focused LLM.

Benefits: Reasoning abilities without any SFT

Challenges: Chains of reasoning have formatting and readability issues

16.13DeepSeek R1 – Training Recipe

DeepSeek R1s is trained as follow

Step 1: Pretrain with traditional techniques → V3-Base

As with R1-Zero, training starts by pretraining a large language model on massive text data using next-token prediction. This produces V3-Base, which has strong general language understanding but no specialized reasoning ability yet.

Step 2: Small-scale SFT with reasoning data

The model is then fine-tuned using a small supervised dataset focused on reasoning.
The data comes from long chains of thought generated by R1-Zero, which are rewritten and cleaned by humans. This step helps the model learn high-quality reasoning structure and formatting in a controlled way.

Step 3: GRPO with reasoning data (same as R1-Zero)

Next, reinforcement learning (GRPO) is applied on reasoning tasks such as math, coding, and logic.
The model is rewarded for:

  • Correct answers

  • Clear reasoning format

  • Language consistency

This step strengthens reasoning ability beyond supervised learning.

Step 4: Large-scale SFT with reasoning + non-reasoning data (~200k pairs)

The model is then trained on a large supervised dataset that includes

  • Reasoning tasks

  • General tasks (QA, chat, writing, etc.)

Most of this data is reused from V3 SFT, ensuring the model remains useful for everyday tasks, not just reasoning.

Step 5: GRPO with reasoning and general data → DeepSeek R1

Finally, GRPO is applied again on both

  • Reasoning tasks (math, coding, logic)

    • Reward: formatting + accuracy

  • General tasks (chat, assistance)

    • Reward: helpfulness + harmlessness

This final step balances strong reasoning with safe and helpful general behavior, producing DeepSeek R1.

16.14Distillation

Distillation (Model Distillation) is a technique used to transfer knowledge from a large, powerful model to a smaller or simpler model.

A big model teaches a smaller model how to behave.

  • Teacher model → large, accurate (e.g., DeepSeek R1)

  • Student model → smaller, faster, cheaper

How Distillation Works (Steps)

  • Teacher generates outputs

The large model answers many questions (often with reasoning or explanations).

  • Student learns from teacher outputs

Instead of learning only from ground-truth labels, the student learns to imitate the teacher’s responses.

  • Match behavior, not just answers

The student learns

  • What answer to give

  • How confident to be

  • Sometimes how to reason or format outputs

Why Distillation Is Useful

  • Smaller model

  • Faster inference

  • Lower cost

  • Retains much of the teacher’s performance

Types of Distillation (Quick)

  • Logit distillation: student matches teacher’s probability outputs

  • Response distillation: student copies teacher’s final answers

  • Reasoning/CoT distillation: student learns step-by-step reasoning

In DeepSeek Context

  • R1-Zero / R1 can act as teacher

  • Smaller models are distilled from them to get reasoning ability at lower cost
